Touch is a fundamentally human gesture that reflects not just our capacity for compassion and care, but also of resistance and aggression. Touch is an important device in the performing arts – musicians have a certain touch on their instruments, and dancers in contact improvisation use body collisions in their choreography.

Body and touch are products of a hybrid construction: even in early modern times, the sense of time through feeling the pulse created rhythmic communication in polyphonic music. In contrast to modern conducting or mechanical methods of timekeeping, these points of contact were places of participation, of shared time, of negotiating power. The role of costumes and garments in early modern opera and theatre corresponded to interaction found in polyphonic music.

In the contemporary everyday, touch has been appropriated by the technologies of late Western capitalism, making human contact normative and reductive. Smartphones use touchscreens and biometrics to identify and track us. Digital touch, we argue, is brittle and lacks subtlety and sociality. How can we recover the sensuous, expressive dimension of touch? We will experiment with technologies of physiological sensing and conductive thread to create new instruments and musical performance systems. Our aim is to understand what changes when wearable touch technologies merge with artificially intelligent communication systems, drawing from knowledge across philosophy, embodied cognition, interactive music and fashion studies.


## Research Objectives

Our objectives are to: 
- Explore group behaviour in music, and build up a hermeneutics of ‘feel.’ 
- Develop technologies informed by theoretical understandings of touch. 
- Propose transferable methodologies for assessing AI supported touch technologies facilitating interaction between people, and between people and computational systems. 
- Re-enact and extend musical practices with interactive touch systems to understand existing cultural practices and allowing new practices to emerge.
